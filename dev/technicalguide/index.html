Technical Guide

The guide starts with the two-word representation and arithmetic, then follows that arithmetic into scalar functions, vector evaluation, and linear algebra. See Adding New Functions for guidance on extending the package.

Number representation and arithmetic

Representation as an unevaluated sum

A DoubleFloat{T} holds two floats, hi and lo, whose unevaluated sum is the represented value. In a normalized pair, the low word is bounded by half the high word's ulp(hi).

\[\mathrm{value} = \mathrm{hi} + \mathrm{lo}, \qquad |\mathrm{lo}| \le \frac{\operatorname{ulp}(\mathrm{hi})}{2}.\]

hi is the value rounded to T; lo is the leading part of the rounding error. For Double64 this gives a nominal 106-bit significand (2 × 53), about 32 decimal digits. The reports use 2^-104 as a relative working-precision unit. See Representation and precision for the supported types.

Two structural consequences matter throughout the package:

  • The exponent range is that of T. Overflow and underflow happen on the scale of the underlying type.
  • Precision degrades near the bottom of the range. When |value| < ~4e-292, the lo word is subnormal and the effective precision tapers below 106 bits; below ~1e-308 only Float64 precision remains. This is a representation limit — visible, for example, in exp(x) for x < -670 — and no algorithm returning a Double64 can beat it.

Error-free transformations

Scalar arithmetic uses building blocks from src/math/errorfree.jl. Their error-free identities assume finite inputs and representable intermediate results and residuals.

  • two_sum(a, b) / two_hilo_sum(a, b): hi + lo == a + b exactly.
  • two_prod(a, b): hi + lo == a * b exactly, using fma.
  • Division and square root use these plus one Newton-like correction (src/math/ops/) to recover low bits. Operations returning one DoubleFloat can still round.

A zero low word describes the input pair. The output can still need a low word, as happens when taking the reciprocal of Double64(3.0).

Scalar functions

Elementary function algorithms

Exponential and logarithmic functions

The guarded Double64 paths are described below. The wider exponential paths use either the 16-entry base-2 table or the original reduction with three squarings near the exponent limits. Arguments safely past the zero-rounding threshold skip the polynomial. In the underflow band, both components determine the nearest point on the underlying grid. Directed MPFR bounds refine a rounding decision near a midpoint. Ordinary Double32 exp uses the guarded Float64 route when it can represent the input exactly. Its fallback preserves the two input words and rounds directly to the Float32 grid in the underflow band.

Outside the guarded Double64 log ranges, the 32-center table path rescales tiny inputs and checks both source and destination exponent fields before shifting the low component. log10 also uses this base-2 kernel and converts its result to base ten. The Accuracy Report presents ordinary error and lattice rounding separately.

Cube root and fused multiply-add

Scalar cbrt returns a DoubleFloat. For extreme Double64 inputs it scales both limbs by 2^(-3k) before Newton refinement and scales the root by 2^k afterward. This keeps intermediate products in range and retains the input's low-limb contribution at working precision. Near cancellation, fma and muladd for three Double64 operands refine the result with an allocation-free expansion. Its tuple capacity grows one slot at a time as product and addend terms are inserted.

Trigonometric functions

The scalar kernels in trig.jl reduce arguments with mod2pi and use intervals such as [0, π/32] with tabulated sin and cos anchors and short Taylor kernels. Reductions near the roots (x - π/2, x - π) carry a triple-double π to preserve the low word. Each Taylor kernel has a bounded interval |r| ≤ π/32.

Hyperbolic and inverse hyperbolic functions

The general hyperbolic kernels use exp(±x). The sinh and cosh kernels switch to a Float128 route above 709. Their results remain finite through ln(2·floatmax) ≈ 710.476, beyond the overflow threshold of the intermediate exp.

The inverse hyperbolic kernels in archyptrig.jl preserve relative precision near sensitive points and avoid squaring large arguments.

acsch, asech, acoth delegate with exactly-formed intermediates (e.g. acoth = ½·log1p(2/(x-1))), never through a bare inv(x) where that would lose 1/x - 1.

Special functions

For agm and ellipk in src/math/special/, the AGM iteration stops when the gap |a-b| stops shrinking. This stopping rule tracks the roundoff floor at the operand scale.

The error, gamma, and Bessel functions use Float128 from Quadmath. Its 113-bit significand exceeds Double64 working precision.

Faster Double64 elementary kernels

The kernels below use a short reduced interval or a bounded direct approximation. The extra speed comes from fewer double-word operations and less reconstruction. Near a root, pole, or format limit, the accuracy of the reduced argument or the available output spacing determines which path is appropriate.

Exponentials and logarithms

exp and exp2 use two-component values of 2^(j/128). For exp, a high-word estimate selects the integer index. A three-part ln(2)/128 then reduces the original argument in natural units, including its low component. The first product is exact in the admitted range because its constant head is shortened. exp2 uses an exact dyadic center and a polynomial in base-2 units instead. Both evaluate a degree-nine polynomial and reconstruct with one double-word multiplication and one exact power-of-two scale. The fast input-head ranges are [-670, 700] for exp and [-966, 1023] for exp2. The wider and low-range paths retain their original reduction and subnormal rounding. The 128-entry table leaves more accuracy margin and uses half the storage of the evaluated 256-entry alternative.

expm1 carries the exponential offset from one for Double64 heads with magnitude through one. A tiny argument returns directly before any halving can quantize away the input. Otherwise the reduced polynomial omits its constant term and three fused updates q ← 2q + q² carry the offset through reconstruction. If the reduction selects an integer n, the final expression is (2^n - 1) + 2^n*q. For n = 0, the result is simply q.

log and log2 use 128 exact dyadic mantissa centers and store anchors in the requested base for heads between 2^-500 and 2^500. They evaluate an odd series on t = (m-c)/(m+c). Normalizing the numerator before the reciprocal refinement is essential when its high component is small. Using the upper binade's anchors relative to the next power of two keeps the exponent contribution and residual from nearly cancelling. In the near-one interval, the numerator x - 1 is formed directly. A second residual correction accounts for the tail of the denominator and the rounding of the first quotient correction.

log1p shares the compensated quotient with numerator x and denominator 2 + x for Double64 heads with magnitude through 0.125. The outer part of this interval retains more terms and more double-word coefficients. Evaluating the larger interval with the narrower taper gave measurable errors. Forming x or x - 1 directly also lets the natural logarithm return the first-order term when it is too small to halve without losing representable bits.

Trigonometric and hyperbolic approximations

atan reduces by reciprocal for magnitudes above one and uses 17 dyadic anchors at j/16 on the unit interval. The tangent subtraction formula leaves a reduced argument of magnitude about 1/32. Nine odd correction terms replace the former square-root half-angle step. The highest-degree terms use one component and the remaining terms retain two. Tiny inputs return directly, preserving the sign and avoiding underflow from an intermediate halving.

sinh, cosh, tanh, and tan use direct bounded approximations. sinh and cosh evaluate odd and even Taylor series for magnitudes through one, with one-component coefficients only in the highest terms. The tangent functions use the same rational correction derived from the continued fraction for tanh(x)/x. They change the sign of the squared argument to select circular or hyperbolic tangent. Keeping the correction separate from x protects relative accuracy at zero. The rational ranges extend to one for tanh and one-half for tan. Outside that interval, tan keeps its circular reduction where the reduced sine and cosine are safely away from zero. The wider path uses Float128 when it can store both input components exactly. Otherwise a local MPFR calculation includes the low component in the argument reduction.

Compensated arithmetic and retained low components were chosen before relaxing the error target. Boundary tests vary the low component around each reduction or branch switch. The tests use 2.2 report units for ordinary nonzero results and nearest rounding on the underlying grid for the tested quantized results. These checks and the Accuracy Report give sampled errors. The implementation plan describes the review decisions and the basis for the performance comparisons.

Faster Double32 elementary kernels

A Double32 value consists of two Float32 words. For ordinary scalar calls on this format, a single Float64 evaluation provides additional working precision when the input conversion is exact. The guard bounds the head and the magnitude of the low word, then subtracts the head from the Float64 sum to check that this recovers the low word exactly. The output is split into a rounded Float32 head and its residual, rounded to Float32.

Exact zero arguments to the odd functions and log1p keep their stored sign. The native Double32 batches use the same head ranges and work directly on both Float32 words.

Vector evaluation

Elementary vector kernels

The User Guide describes DoubleFloats.simdmap!, including axes, overlapping inputs, and exceptions. The driver checks storage and element types once per call. The batch kernels reuse the scalar coefficients and error-free transforms without reassociation or @fastmath. They operate on the ordinary interleaved arrays of high and low limbs.

For a native route, the driver checks both limbs in blocks of up to 32 or 64 elements, depending on the operation. The intervals below bound the high word. The low word must be finite and at most half the high word's binade ulp in magnitude. The bounds leave room for the low limb and nested intermediate operations. Vectors shorter than 64 elements take the scalar path.

Mixed blocks normally use the scalar loop, which preserves exception order. The circular kernels prefer the small-angle route and then try the wider circular reduction. The wider route reuses the three-limb π reductions and resolves the table sector using both input limbs. For exp2, ordinary expm1, sin, cos, tan, and wider cis, a block with at most one eighth of its lanes excluded can evaluate safe substitute inputs with SIMD and then repair those lanes with the original scalar calls. This requires every excluded input to pass a wider check that makes the scalar calls and assignments safe. Blocks containing a potential domain error go through the scalar loop before any element of the block is written.

The pi-scaled trig functions, binary atan and DoubleFloats.atanxy, and the inverse circular functions other than unary Double64 and Double32 atan use the existing scalar algorithms inside the batch. The same applies to complex sqrt, log, and the inverse complex functions. This preserves special values, signed zeros, argument quadrants, and complex branch cuts. Threaded calls use contiguous chunks. Most supported operations are eligible from 4,096 elements. For Double64, hypot and paired normalize are eligible from 16,384 elements. Reciprocal and integer power use a 65,536-element threshold.

Native Double32 batches

The six native batch kernels use the same head ranges as their scalar counterparts. They operate directly on both Float32 words, including sparse low words that would be rounded away in a Float64 sum. atan reduces around the nearest dyadic center j/16 and evaluates a short odd polynomial. sinh uses a tapered odd Taylor series. tan and tanh share a shortened continued fraction, with the sign of the squared argument distinguishing the two functions. log2 reuses the existing Float32 table and returns exact integers on powers of two. log1p uses a compensated quotient t = x/(2+x) followed by a five-term odd series for the natural logarithm. Its short interval keeps abs(t) < 0.033, allowing the three highest coefficients to use single Float32 words.

The shared driver uses a native loop on eligible dense blocks and scalar calls elsewhere. expm1 benefits from the guarded scalar route in batches. A prototype using repeated Float32 doubling was slower and exceeded the 2.2-unit accuracy target. The Accuracy Report compares the six production kernels with high-precision references. The implementation plan records the validation criteria.

To be well-balanced

Choose the error measure for the part of the domain you care about. For ordinary finite results away from zero, the Accuracy Report gives relative error in working-precision units. The 99th percentile describes the upper tail of a sample, and the maximum checks the worst case encountered in that sample. Near exponential underflow, compare against the underlying format's rounding grid. Test signed zeros, domain errors, and values near poles separately.

For scalar calls, the main question is whether the fast evaluation preserves the part of the input that matters. Ordinary Double32 calls can use Float64 when it retains both words exactly. Near one, a low word can determine the entire logarithm, so the two-word path is important. The Double64 results show two ways to gain speed. On the sampled intervals, the shorter sinh evaluation lowered both time and maximum error. The finer exp2 reduction lowered time while raising the sampled maximum from 0.837 to 1.009 report units. A 1.0-unit target and a 2.2-unit target give different assessments of that change.

For vector calls, compare the batch with current scalar evaluation on the same inputs. In a matched run with 32,768 elements on one i9-14900K thread using Julia 1.10.10, the eleven Double64 operations listed in the scalar benchmarks above gained 2.96–3.59× on dense eligible inputs and produced identical output words. The six native Double32 kernels used two-Float32 arithmetic with higher sampled errors than their scalar paths.

An occasional out-of-range input can send a whole block to scalar evaluation. With independent inputs, a whole-block kernel of width m stays eligible with probability p^m, where p is the per-element eligibility rate. With 1% scattered misses, about 72.5% of 32-element blocks are expected to remain eligible. The matched run measured 72.3%. Grouping inputs in compatible regimes changes that fraction. The repair path for Double64 exp2 kept all blocks on a native route in its mixed fixture and retained a 2.86× speedup. Storage, overlap, and threaded exception order also affect which routes are available.

A practical balance starts by screening for required semantics and hard error limits. In this fixture, doubling throughput with a sampled maximum below 2.2 units favored dense Double32 log1p, sinh, and tanh. Their mixed runs fell below 2×. A 1.0-unit limit already excludes the dense log1p batch on the sampled data.

For candidates that pass the screen, take the larger of the 𝐏₉₉ error divided by its target and the maximum error divided by its target as a normalized error score. If the batch is faster and has a higher score, let $s$ be its speedup and $r$ its score divided by the scalar score. The crossover weight on accuracy is

\[w^* = \frac{\log s}{\log s + \log r}.\]

With 𝐏₉₉ and maximum targets of 1 and 2.2 units, the switch for Double32 tanh occurs at 0.51 for the dense fixture and at 0.38 for the mixed fixture. Below the crossover the model favors the batch. Above it the model favors the scalar loop. This weight expresses a workload preference.

Use input ranges, storage, and sizes representative of your own work. The matched comparison and crossover model gives the function-by-function data, fixture details, and algebra for choosing different speed and accuracy targets.

Linear algebra

Factorizations and matrix equations

LAPACK covers only Float32/64 and their complexes, so DoubleFloats routes dense factorizations through pure-Julia generic implementations:

  • lu, qr, \: LinearAlgebra's generic kernels.
  • eigen, eigvals, schur, hessenberg: GenericSchur (QR iteration). DoubleFloats owns explicit eigen/eigvals methods (src/math/linearalgebra/eigen.jl) that pin this routing, detect hermitian input (tridiagonalization + symmetric QR: real eigenvalues, orthonormal vectors), and normalize output conventions to match the LAPACK route (real Eigen for a real spectrum, (real, imag) sorting, unit-norm vectors).
  • svd: GenericLinearAlgebra.
  • sylvester/lyap (sylvester.jl): Bartels–Stewart — Schur factorizations of both operands, triangular back-substitution, transform back.
  • lq: the generic Householder QR of the adjoint, repackaged as an LQ factorization (A = L·Q ⟺ A′ = Q_qr·R), with the missing LQPackedQ materialization/multiplication kernels supplied for DoubleFloat element types.

Matrix functions

See Linear Algebra for function domains and a worked example. The dedicated implementations in src/math/linearalgebra/matfun.jl can handle defective inputs. Structure checks apply to the stored matrix. For a skipped entry, both limbs of each component must be zero.

Polynomial evaluation and triangular structure

  • Exponential and trig/hyperbolic families. The exponential uses scaling and squaring with a degree-34 Taylor polynomial evaluated by Paterson–Stockmeyer. For ordinary real Double64 input with a positive one-norm up to 2^-7, it uses degree 15. The circular and hyperbolic pairs share their powers and apply double-angle formulas. cos and cosh use the even polynomial alone.
  • Triangular products. For eligible dense Double64 and ComplexDF64 triangles from size 64 and with input one-norm at most 8, the polynomial and reconstruction products skip structurally zero entries. The products retain ascending accumulation order and check both limbs before using the packed arithmetic. Lower triangles are handled by transpose. tan and tanh also use triangular right division when the computed denominator is triangular and its distance from identity in the one-norm is at most 0.5.

Square roots, logarithms, and powers

  • Square root. A triangular input uses the Björck–Hammarling recurrence on the original matrix. In the near-identity envelope ‖T - I‖₁ ≤ 0.625, eligible triangles from size 96 use blocks of width 12. Rectangular updates pack directly from views, reusing per-call buffers, and the off-diagonal blocks are obtained by triangular Sylvester solves. The point recurrence handles small and excluded cases.
  • Logarithm. The triangular route takes square roots until ‖T - I‖₁ ≤ 1/4 or the iteration cap is reached. It forms G = (T - I)/(T + I) by a triangular right solve and evaluates the atanh series. For eligible triangles, products use dense packed multiplication from size 24 and triangular packed multiplication from size 64. The inverse scaling uses the blocked square root whenever the current factor qualifies.
  • Schur reconstruction. General sqrt and log use complex Schur factors. Eligible ComplexDF64 factors from size 16 use a materialized adjoint in the packed reconstruction. The real-input result still goes through the usual imaginary-roundoff check.
  • Fractional powers. The usual exp(p * log(A)) route can use the triangular optimizations in both stages. For ordinary nonsingular real Double64 triangles in the near-identity envelope above, an exact exponent of one-half uses sqrt directly. The existing logarithm branch handles singular cases.

Diagonal and symmetric matrices

Ordinary real Double64 diagonal inputs use scalar diagonal evaluations for exp, sqrt, log, and the sine/cosine and hyperbolic sine/cosine families when the absolute diagonal heads are on [2^-64, 8]. The sqrt and log diagonal routes require positive entries. For ordinary real symmetric inputs from size 32 with one-norm at most 0.875, exp and the same paired and single-output trig and hyperbolic functions use the polynomial drivers. Other eligible symmetric/Hermitian inputs use eigendecomposition.

The general routes retain the type and branch conventions for sqrt, log, and matrix powers. cbrt, sinpi/cospi, and the matrix inverse circular and hyperbolic functions still use eigen-diagonalization, which requires a diagonalizable argument.
